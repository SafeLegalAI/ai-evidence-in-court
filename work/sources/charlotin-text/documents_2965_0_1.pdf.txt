               25        Defendants contend meets the standard of sealing set by the Ninth Circuit in Kamakana v. City &
               26        Cnty. Of Honolulu, 447 F.3d 1172 (9th Cir. 2006) and its progeny. Defendants also rely on NRS
               27        289.025, which is titled: “Confidentiality of home address and photograph of peace officer in

 8   language of NRS 289.025 and, therefore, the images of the police officers in that footage must

10   contend that the release of the body worn camera images serves no benefit to the public and, thus,

11   the Kamakana standard is met.

12          Plaintiff contends that there is a strong presumption of disclosure under Kamakana and

13   follow-on Ninth Circuit case law because the body worn camera video was altered and fabricated to

14   create false appearances. Plaintiff avers this fraud is of particular public concern in light of the

15   increasing risks of deepfake technology. Plaintiff says the images on the body worn camera are

16   “deepfake alterations deliberately introduced by defense counsel to mislead the Court.” ECF No. 96

17   at 5. Plaintiff attacks defense counsel and says there is no legitimate privacy interest at stake.

18          With respect to NRS 289.025, Plaintiff concludes it does not govern federal proceedings and

19   responds to Defendants’ public interest argument by contending that allegations of fabricated body

20   worn camera footage outweighs any “generalized” privacy interest the law enforcement officers may

21   have. Plaintiff says keeping the footage confidential is prejudicial to him and his case because

22   sealing these images will only lead to more motion practice and, in any event, Plaintiff is trying to

23   bring to light the issue of fabrication, which “federal authorities” will want to review for purposes

24   of criminal prosecutions. Plaintiff closes his argument by again attacking defense counsel as

25   misrepresenting facts to the Court. Plaintiff seeks sanctions.

26          Defendants respond to Plaintiff’s arguments reiterating the applicable standard and quoting

27   the NRS at issue. Defendants then argue that Plaintiff’s repeated accusations of altered body worn

28   camera images is unsupported by any evidence and that NRS 289.025 is clearly applicable to the

13   II.    Discussion

14          The Court notes that the intent and application of NRS 289.025 has not been discussed by

15   the Nevada Court of Appeals or the Nevada Supreme Court. The District of Nevada applied the

16   statute in Sykes v. Las Vegas Metropolitan Police Department, Case No. 2:21-cv-1479-RFB-DJA,

17   2023 WL 9896903, at *1 (D. Nev. Oct. 25, 2023), and withheld a police officer’s home address from

18   public disclosure under NRS 289.025. In Voss v. McClellan, Case No. 3:08-cv-0342-RJC (RAM),

19   2011 WL 1076135, at *10 (D. Nev Feb. 8, 2011), the Court again held that disclosure of a police

20   officer’s home address was impermissible under the statute. This Court notes that withholding a law

21   enforcement officer’s home address in support of safety does not necessarily equate to withholding

22   body worn camera video. Nonetheless, the Court in McClellan did take note that, within the prison

23   system, disclosing personal information of corrections officers could endanger the safety and

24   security of such officers. Id. While the Court welcomes the application of NRS 289.025 in these

25   cases, neither decision addressed body worn camera footage.

26          First, the Court finds Plaintiff offers no evidence that supports the conclusion that Metro

27   altered or fabricated body worn camera footage. While this issue may be resolved through this

28   proceeding, and Plaintiff need not disprove the request to seal is appropriate, the Court nevertheless

                                                      3
 1   notes that there is nothing at this juncture on which it can rely with confidence to conclude Plaintiff’s

 2   allegation is true or even worthy of credence such that there is a public interest in viewing alleged

 3   altered or fabricated police video. Second, the Court categorically rejects Plaintiff’s unsupported

 4   allegations regarding defense counsel’s conduct as the counsel appearing in this case are officers of

 5   the Court and their representations are taken as true in the absence of any reason to question what

 6   they have proffered. Third, that the body worn camera footage contains images of the officers is

 7   without doubt. Thus, there is some basis to find NRS 289.025 was meant to prevent disclosure of

 8   camera footage; albeit this issue is unresolved. Fourth, in contrast to Plaintiff’s current position,

 9   Plaintiff previously told the Court he recognized the risks associated with disclosure including

10   “attracting unnecessary public interest if publicly disseminated” and “unfairly affect[ing] the

11   integrity of these proceedings.” ECF No. 72 at 2. Plaintiff’s representation regarding the impact of

12   disclosure was made only (approximately) six weeks ago on August 21, 2025. Id.

13          Plaintiff’s recognition that release of video could unfairly affect the integrity of these

14   proceedings, a concern Defendants adopt, is considered along with Defendants’ contention that

15   release may cause efforts to “dox” the individual Defendants (that is, prompt a search for and

16   malicious publication of private information about the officers being sued), especially in light of the

17   allegations made by this pro se Plaintiff. The Court finds these concerns sufficiently establish

18   compelling reasons to permit the body worn camera footage to be filed under seal. While the public’s

19   interest in the integrity of law enforcement activity is not discounted, the safety of law enforcement

20   and potential misuse of the video (given the unique aspects of the current case that includes

21   Plaintiff’s salacious allegations of egregious wrongdoing (see ECF No. 7)) is of equal importance.

22          This is not mere generalized concern, but recognition of specific danger impacting the

23   integrity of the instant proceedings and safety of the individual Defendants, which must override the

24   disclosure of the evidence at issue especially given the lack of credible information that would allow

25   the Court to find a basis to conclude the body worn video was fabricated or materially altered. It is

26   these considerations and not necessarily the language of NRS 289.025, which compels the Court’s

27   decision. The Court notes this decision is not static; that is, should information come to light
