    1                                         I. BACKGROUND
    2          On March 11, 2025, Plaintiffs moved for summary judgment in this action. In
    3    support, Plaintiffs provided several documents as exhibits. Upon review, the Court
    4    suspected Plaintiffs’ exhibits 3, 6A, 6B, 6C, 7, 21, 27, 35, and 40 of having been altered or
    5    created by generative artificial intelligence (GenAI). For instance, exhibits 6A, 6C, and 36
    6    purport to capture the speech and image of Geri Haas. But, while exhibit 36 appears to
    7    capture a real-life interaction, certain characteristics of exhibits 6A and 6C, such as the
    8    lack of facial expressions, the looping video feed, among other things, suggested that these
    9    exhibits were products of GenAI—i.e., “deepfakes.”

    13   reinforced when Maridol Mendones mentioned that some witnesses depicted in the suspect

    10   B. Appropriate Sanction

    11           The Court next turns to the appropriate sanction. In determining the appropriate

    12   sanction, the Court is guided by section 128.7(d)’s guidance that “[a] sanction imposed for

    13   violation of subdivision (b) shall be limited to what is sufficient to deter repetition of this

    14   conduct or comparable conduct by others similarly situated.” (§ 128.7(d).)

    15
                 1. Monetary Sanctions
    16
                 The Court finds that monetary sanctions are not appropriate.
    17
                 The Court is attentive to the issue of improper use of GenAI before it. (See, e.g.,
    18
         Evans v. Execushield, Inc. (Cal. Super. Ct. Mar. 10, 2025) 2025 WL 893084 (discussing the
    19
         presentation of fake citations to the court by attorneys).) The misuse of GenAI in legal
    20
         proceedings most frequently happens in the context of presenting fictitious citations. (See
    21
         U.S. v. Hayes (E.D. Cal. 2025) 763 F. Supp. 3d 1054.) In that context, appropriate
    22
         sanctions include (1) penalties payable to the court in the range of $1,000.00 to $5,000.00,
    23
         (2) sending a copy of the order to the client, (3) sending a copy of the order to each judge
    24
         falsely identified as the author of a fake opinion, (4) sending a copy of the order to all the
    25
         district judges and magistrate judges in the district, and (5) sending a copy of the order to
    26
         the relevant bar association. (Id. at 1073; see also Mata v. Avianca, Inc. (S.D.N.Y. 2023)
    27
         678 F. Supp. 3d 443, 466 (imposing similar sanctions); Gjovik v. Apple Inc. (N.D. Cal., May
    28

                                                        15
                                        Order re Terminating Sanctions
1
    1    19, 2025, No. 23-CV-04597-EMC) 2025 WL 1447380, at *7 (“At this juncture, the Court
    2    shall not impose any sanctions. However, it forewarns Ms. Gjovik that (1) she is
    3    responsible for verifying the accuracy of AI-generated or AI-provided information,
    4    including but not limited to case citations and content, and that (2) failure to do so may
    5    lead to sanctions, including but not limited to a finding of contempt and/or the ability to
    6    proceed pro se.”).)
    7          The reasons for imposing these sanctions were captured by the Hayes court:
    8    “Submitting fictitious cases and quotations to the court ‘degrades or impugns the integrity
    9    of the Court’ and ‘interferes with the administration of justice’ in violation of Local Rule
    10   180(e), and violates California Rules of Professional Conduct 3.1(a)(2), 3.3(a)(1), and
    11   3.3(a)(2).” (See also Hayes, 763 F. Supp. 3d at 1064.)
    12         Here, the use of GenAI went beyond the submission of fictitious citations. Plaintiffs
    13   submitted at least two exhibits created by GenAI. Further, to an even greater extent than
    14   expressed by the Hayes court, the use of deepfakes in a case significantly undermines the
    15   Court’s ability to administer justice, significantly erodes the public’s confidence in the
    16   judicial system, and significantly burdens under-resourced and overworked courts with
    17   the time-consuming task of assessing whether evidence presented to it during pretrial
    18   proceedings was a deepfake. As such, a more severe sanction is appropriate.
    19

    26         of the cases in which the oath may by law of the State of California be

    27         matter which he or she knows to be false, and every person who testifies,
               declares, deposes, or certifies under penalty of perjury in any of the cases in
    28         which the testimony, declarations, depositions, or certification is permitted by

               law of the State of California under penalty of perjury and willfully states as
    1          true any material matter which he or she knows to be false, is guilty of perjury.
    2

    4    (h) of Section 1170 for two, three or four years.” (§ 126.)

    6
               Every person who, with the intent to defraud, knowing that he or she has no
    7          authority to do so, signs the name of another person or of a fictitious person to

    12
         simultaneously too severe and not sufficiently remedial. The sanction is too severe as even
    13
         being the subject of a criminal investigation may lead to social repercussions that persist
    14
         after the criminal proceedings close. This civil judicial officer does not have the expertise
    15
         and experience to balance all relevant considerations to determine whether a matter
    16
         should be referred to the District Attorney for a criminal investigation. At the same time,
    17
         a referral would do little to address the harm that Plaintiffs have caused in this civil
    18
         proceeding.

    20
                       ii. Evidence or Issue Sanctions
    21
               The Court finds that evidence or issue sanctions are not appropriate. This sanction
    22
         would not serve an adequate deterrent against future uses. As illustrated above,
    23
         examining whether evidentiary submissions were created using GenAI is labor intensive,
    24
         and courts (really, the public at large) are not adequately prepared to discern whether a
    25
         person depicted in a video was created using GenAI. (See, e.g., Shannon Bond, AI-
    26
         generated images are everywhere. Here's how to spot them, NPR (June 13, 2023),
    27
         https://www.npr.org/2023/06/07/1180768459/how-to-identify-ai-generated-deepfake-
    28
         images.) While an evidentiary or issue sanction would exclude some things that can be
                                                        17
                                        Order re Terminating Sanctions
1
    1    shown to be created by GenAI, others might sneak through the Court’s or opposing party’s
    2    analysis. Without robust safeguards to guard against future misuses of the legal
    3    proceedings, an evidentiary or issue sanction is effectively toothless.
    4

    5                 iii. Terminating Sanction

    6          The Court finds that a terminating sanction is appropriate. This sanction is

    7    proportional to the harm that Plaintiffs’ misuse of the Court’s processes has caused. A

    8    terminating sanction serves the appropriate remedial effect of denying Plaintiffs— and

    9    other litigants seeking to make use of GenAI to submit video testimonials—of the ability

    10   to further prosecute this action after violating the Court’s and the Defendants’ trust so

    11   egregiously. (See R.S. Creative, Inc. v. Creative Cotton, Ltd. (1999) 75 Cal. App. 4th 486,

    12   497 (The Civil Discovery Act “authorizes terminating sanctions in the first instance in

    13   egregious cases.”).)

    14         Further, a terminating sanction serves the appropriate deterrent effect of showing

    15   the public that the Court has zero tolerance with attempting to pass deepfakes as

    16   evidence.

    17         This sanction serves the appropriately chilling message to litigants appearing

    18   before this Court: Use GenAI in court with great caution.
