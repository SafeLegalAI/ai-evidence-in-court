A. CONTEXT




                                                                                                 2024 QCCS 1654 (CanLII)
[1] Mr. Azubuike was charged in 2020 with a series of crimes arising from the
allegedly fraudulent purchase of used automobiles from COPART Auction (“COPART”).

[2] COPART holds auctions for cars that have been written off for insurance
purposes. Between December 1, 2019, and January 30, 2020, 9359-7201 Quebec Inc.
(“Quebec Inc.”) purchased approximately $300,000.00 worth of vehicles from COPART.
The company submitted a series of counterfeit bank drafts in payment.

[3] Quebec Inc. had been assigned COPART client number [...]. The company had
submitted material which identified Mr. Karen Voskerchyan as its president with a
telephone number - (514) [...] (exhibits P-1, P-2a, P-2b, P-4 and P-5 produced at trial).

[4] Mr. Azubuike was also a client of COPART Auction through his company, Ekens
Foundation International. He had submitted material which identified himself as a
director of the company with a telephone number – (514) 641-2419 (exhibits P-8, P-9
and P-10 produced at trial).

[5] The only live issue at trial is the identity of Mr. Azubuike as the person responsible
for Quebec Inc.’s bids and its supplying of the counterfeit bank drafts. One of the
elements relied upon by the prosecution to prove identity was a series of eight
recordings made to COPART’s customer service center located in Dallas, Texas, on
behalf of client #[...] – Quebec Inc. The calls had been made by a male who identified
himself as Karen Voskerchyan and who provided Quebec Inc.’s client number. They
were made between October 16, 2019, and November 25, 2019, inclusively. They were
made using telephone number (514) [...] which was identified in COPART’s files to Mr.
Azubuike and his company (exhibit P-11, documents 1 and 2 produced at trial). The
lead investigator in the case, Lt.Det. Mélanie Gagnon, testified at trial that her familiarity
with Mr. Azubuike’s voice led her to believe that the male voice on the recordings was
his.

[6] The recordings had been provided to the prosecution on February 13, 2020
(exhibit P-11, document 2, produced at trial). By notice dated January 10, 2024,
Mr. Azubuike was advised that the prosecution would be producing the recordings at
trial (exhibit J-2, Tab 1).

[7] Since Mr. Azubuike was representing himself, the Court provided him information
on the trial process during a management hearing held on January 17, 2024. During
that session, Mr. Azubuike was advised that he had to be vigilant should he desire to
present a defence (exhibit J-1, Tab 7, paragr. 6, 7 and 8).

[8] On April 22, 2024, Mr. Azubuike’s trial on one count charging fraud began with the
formation of the jury. At the end of the day, on April 26, 2024, the Court held a first in-

B. THE VOIR-DIRE

[13] Justice Watt, on behalf of the Court of Appeal of Ontario, has helpfully summarized
the principles related to the purpose of the voir-dire seeking the production of expert
evidence in the case of R. v. Evans, 2019 ONCA 715:

     133.   The authorities teach that the admissibility of expert opinion evidence is to be
            determined in accordance with a two-step analysis. (…)

     134.   At the first step of the analysis, it falls to the proponent of the evidence to establish,
            on the balance of probabilities, the threshold requirements of admissibility. These
            include, but are not limited to, relevance and a properly qualified expert. (…)

     137.   Expert evidence on a subject must be given by an expert, that is to say, by a
            witness who is shown to have acquired special or peculiar knowledge of a subject
            through study or experience. Such evidence can only assist the trier of fact to the
            extent that the witness has acquired special knowledge about that subject that the
            average trier of fact lacks (…) If the proposed expert’s special or peculiar
            knowledge of the subject on which he or she is tended to testify is minimal, he or
            she should not be qualified as an expert on the subject. (…)

     138.   At the second step, the gatekeeper stage of the admissibility analysis, the judge
            balances the potential risks and benefits of admitting the expert opinion evidence
            that has satisfied the threshold admissibility requirements. By this balancing, the
            judge decides whether the potential benefits of admitting the evidence justify the
            risks associated with its introduction. The relevance and reliability of the evidence
            and its necessity to assist the trier of fact in the correct disposal of the case, on the
            one hand, are measured against the counterweights of consumption of time,
            prejudice and confusion, on the other. The reliability versus effect factor is of
            special importance.

[14] The prosecution relies upon the lead investigator’s identification of Mr. Azubuike’s
voice on the 8 conversations as one evidentiary element to establish his identity as the
perpetrator of the alleged fraud. Mr. Madasar’s expert testimony is proposed to
challenge the investigator’s opinion. He is proposed as an expert in Artificial
Intelligence, specializing in voice cloning and voice analysis. Referring to the first step of
an expert opinion voir-dire, the Court holds that Mr. Mudasar’s proposed testimony
would be prima facie relevant, would be prima facie necessary in assisting the jury, and
would not be the subject of any exclusionary rule. The issue therefore becomes, is he a
properly qualified expert?

[15] The proof led during the voir-dire clearly establishes that Mr. Mudasar is not
remotely qualified to testify as an expert witness on Artificial Intelligence (AI), voice




                                                                                                2024 QCCS 1654 (CanLII)
cloning or voice analysis. He is a pleasant and personable 22-year-old man who is a
resident of Pakistan.

[16] His C.V. (exhibit VD-16-D-4) and testimony establish that, after finishing high
school in 2017 (the C.V.) or 2019 (his testimony), he paused his studies because his
marks were not high enough to contemplate university.

[17] During the pause, he worked with a relative who was free-lancing in the field of AI.
He returned to high school during 2022. His grade improvement permitted him to be
accepted at the University of Sindh as a student pursuing a Bachelor’s degree in Library
and Information Science and Archive Studies. He is in his second year.

[18] He also completed three online courses offered by Vanderbilt University (exhibit V-
16-D-4) in the non-credit specialization of “prompt engineering”. To quote from the
Certificate he received:

          The specialization provides you with principles and templates for
          writing effective prompts that tap into the capabilities of systems like
          ChatGPT. You will gain skills for conversing with AI to harness its
          reasoning and creative potential.

[19] In summary, his educational background provides no basis to qualify Mr. Mudasar
as an expert in detecting or identifying voice cloning.

[20] As for his practical experience gained from working with a relative or free-lancing
on his own, it falls short of providing a basis to qualify him as an expert witness. He
admits that he has used voice cloning software for less than a year, beginning in mid-
2023. He has no special ability in the field of voice cloning as compared to any other
young person who would consult YouTube videos on the subject.

[21] Considering that the Court has concluded that Mr. Mudasar is not a qualified
expert witness, it is not necessary to analyze the non-respect of certain conditions
provided by s.657.3 Cr.C. nor whether a cost/benefit analysis would lead to a
determination of inadmissibility.

AS A RESULT, THE COURT:

[22] DECLARES that Mr. Ahmed Mudasar is not a qualified expert witness in the field
of Artificial Intelligence specializing in voice cloning and voice analysis.

[23] DECLARES the testimony of Mr. Ahmed Mudasar and his report VD-16-D3
inadmissible.
